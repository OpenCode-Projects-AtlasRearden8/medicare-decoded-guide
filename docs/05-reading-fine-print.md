# 5. Reading a Plan's Fine Print

## The six papers that decide everything

Every Advantage and Part D plan produces a stack of official documents.
You do not need to read all of them; you need to know which three matter
and what the sentences mean. Here is the cast:

| Paper | Size | What it is |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Summary of Benefits** | A few pages | The copay-and-premium cheat sheet. The fastest honest summary of a plan. |
| **Evidence of Coverage (EOC)** | The size of a phone book | The legal contract. Read it like a dictionary when something surprises you, not cover to cover. |
| **Annual Notice of Change (ANOC)** | A letter, every fall | What is *changing* next year: costs, formulary, network. The single most important piece of fall mail. |
| **Formulary** | Booklet or PDF | The drug list and its tiers. Whether *your* drugs are covered, at what cost, with what restrictions. |
| **Provider directory** | Varies | Whether your doctors, hospitals, and pharmacies are in the plan's network. |
| **Pharmacy network** | Varies | Some plans pay less (or nothing) unless you use listed pharmacies. |

*Education and paperwork help only — never medical, legal, financial, or
plan advice.*

## If you read nothing else, read these three pages

1. **The ANOC "changes" letter's summary page.** Plans must summarize
   what gets more expensive or more restrictive. This page is the
   difference between "my plan changed" and "my plan changed and I
   noticed."
2. **The Summary of Benefits cost table.** Scan six rows: primary care
   copay, specialist copay, inpatient hospital (per day or per stay),
   outpatient surgery, emergency room, and the yearly out-of-pocket
   maximum.
3. **The formulary rows for your top five drugs.** Which tier? Any
   quantity limits? Prior authorization required? This one page can
   swing your real costs more than the premium does.

## The copay-row decoder

Fine print costs are usually written like this. Here is what the shapes
mean:

| Phrase | Plain meaning | Where surprises hide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "$15 primary / $45 specialist" | Flat copays per visit | Specialist rates also apply to many therapy and procedure visits |
| "$350 per day for days 1–5" | Per-day hospital copay, capped at 5 | A 10-day stay bills like 5 days, not 10 — but a second stay in the year may bill again |
| "$1,500 per stay" | Per-admission copay | Two hospitalizations = two copays |
| "20% coinsurance" | Your percentage of the approved amount | Coinsurance on expensive items (chemotherapy-type drugs, DME) can dwarf a copay |
| "$0" | Nothing for that service — in network | Out-of-network versions may still cost you |
| "up to $50" | A ceiling, not the price | The actual copay may be less |
| " deductible then 25%" | Pay the yearly deductible first, then a percentage | Order matters; the deductible resets every January |

## Fine-print phrases, translated

| Phrase | What it actually means |
| --- | --- |
| Prior authorization | The plan must approve the service or drug *before* you get it, or it may not pay |
| Step therapy | You must try cheaper drugs first, and fail on them, before the plan covers the expensive one |
| Quantity limits | A maximum amount per fill or per month |
| Formulary tiers | 1 = cheapest generics, then brands, then specialty drugs; the tier sets your cost |
| "Non-formulary" | The plan does not cover this drug at all (exceptions can be requested) |
| Out-of-pocket maximum | The yearly ceiling on in-network cost-sharing; after it, the plan pays 100% for covered in-network care |
| "Contracted providers only" | Network doctors only, except emergencies |
| Accepts assignment | The provider agrees to Medicare's approved amount as full payment (Original Medicare term) |
| "Not a benefit, subject to change" | This perk can shrink or vanish next year; the ANOC is the notice |
| Creditable coverage | Drug coverage at least as good as Part D — having it means you can skip D without a late penalty |

## The five questions before comparing any two plans

Answer these on paper (the [comparison worksheet](worksheets/plan-fine-print-comparison-worksheet.md)
gives you the table):

1. **My drugs:** are my top five prescriptions on the formulary, at
   which tiers, with which restrictions?
2. **My doctors:** are my current doctors and hospital in the network
   *for the year being sold*, not last year's?
3. **My pharmacy:** is my pharmacy in the plan's network, and do my
   drugs cost less there than by mail order?
4. **My worst case:** what is the out-of-pocket maximum, and what counts
   toward it?
5. **My travel:** what happens when I am out of the service area for a
   month or a season?

## Star ratings, in one honest paragraph

Medicare rates plans from 1 to 5 stars each fall, measuring service
quality — complaint rates, appeal timeliness, customer service — not how
well the plan fits *you*. Higher stars are generally a good sign about
the plan's operations. Stars do not mean "this plan is right for me,"
and a 4-star plan with your drug off-formulary still costs you real
money. Treat stars as one number on the table, never the headline.

## Red-flag fine print

Pause and re-read when you see:

- **"$0 premium"** with a large per-day hospital copay or high drug
  tiers — the cost moved, it did not vanish.
- **"Network subject to change"** in a plan whose network is the reason
  you chose it — verify your doctors each fall in the new directory.
- **A formulary tier jump** for one of your drugs between this year and
  next year's ANOC — price the new tier before renewing.
- **"Coverage gap" language** — much of the old gap system has been
  replaced by a yearly out-of-pocket drug cap, so old brochures can be
  outdated; trust the newest papers.
- **Anything that only exists in the ad** and not in the Summary of
  Benefits. Ads are not contracts; summaries are.
