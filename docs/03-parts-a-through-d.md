# 3. Parts A–D, Translated

## The letters are just buckets

Medicare's alphabet is not an exam; it is four buckets that different
kinds of care fall into. Once you can sort care into buckets, every letter
in every brochure starts to mean something.

*Education and paperwork help only — never medical, legal, financial, or
plan advice.*

## Part A — the hospital part

**What it is for:** care where you are *formally admitted* — inpatient
hospital stays, short stays in a skilled nursing facility (rehab, not
long-term care), hospice, and some home health.

**How it behaves, in teaching numbers:**

- Premium: usually **$0** for people who (or whose spouses) worked and
  paid Medicare taxes for about ten years. If not, a monthly premium
  applies.
- Big deductible: imagine **$1,500 per benefit period** (a benefit period
  starts the day you're admitted and ends 60 days after you leave — so
  multiple hospitalizations in a short stretch share one deductible).
- After the deductible: **days 1–60 cost $0 per day**, then a per-day
  rate climbs for longer stays. Skilled nursing works similarly with a
  higher day rate after day 20.
- Surprise to know about: **observation status is not admission.** Hours
  spent "under observation" bill under Part B, which changes what you
  owe — hospitals must give you a notice explaining this. Ask "am I an
  inpatient or under observation?" if a stay stretches out.

## Part B — the doctors part

**What it is for:** outpatient care — doctor visits (including surgery
done outpatient), lab tests, imaging, medical equipment, physical
therapy, mental health visits, and preventive services like the yearly
wellness visit and screenings.

**How it behaves, in teaching numbers:**

- Premium: a monthly amount **everyone pays** (roughly $170 in teaching
  numbers; higher for high incomes — the surcharge is called IRMAA).
- Deductible: a small one, imagine **$250 a year**.
- After the deductible: **you pay 20% of approved amounts, forever, with
  no cap.** That open-ended 20% is precisely the "gap" that supplements
  fill and that Advantage plans restructure — the entire rest of this
  guide orbits that sentence.
- Doctors who "accept assignment" agree to take Medicare's approved
  amount as full payment; the 20% is the most they can bill you.

## Part C — not a benefit, a delivery

**What it is:** Medicare Advantage — a private plan that bundles Parts A
and B (and almost always D) into one plan with one card. Part C does not
*add* benefits on top of Original Medicare; it *replaces the delivery* of
A and B with a private plan that must cover everything A and B cover.

**How it behaves, in teaching numbers:**

- Premium: often **$0 on top of your Part B premium** (you always keep
  paying for B in both doors).
- Costs come as copays and coinsurance from a schedule: $15 primary
  visit, $45 specialist, a per-day hospital copay, and so on.
- Networks: an HMO generally requires using its network (with
  exceptions for emergencies); a PPO allows out-of-network care at a
  higher price.
- Rules: plans may require **prior authorization** (approval before a
  service) and **referrals** to see specialists.
- The plan must set an **out-of-pocket maximum** — a yearly ceiling on
  how much you can be asked to pay in-network. Original Medicare has no
  such cap (that is what Medigap is for).
- Extras: dental, vision, hearing, gym perks, and the like vary by plan
  and change yearly. Perks are marketing terms; the Summary of Benefits
  is the contract.

## Part D — the drugs part

**What it is:** prescription drug coverage, sold by private insurers,
either standalone (with Original Medicare) or bundled into an Advantage
plan.

**How it behaves, in teaching numbers:**

- Premium: plan-by-plan, imagine **$35 a month**.
- Drug lists called **formularies** group drugs into **tiers** —
  tier 1 cheapest (generics), tier 2 preferred brands, higher tiers
  costlier and sometimes requiring special approval.
- You pay copays or coinsurance per fill, plus possibly a deductible.
- Since recent federal changes, there is a **yearly cap on your
  out-of-pocket drug spending** (in the low thousands of dollars; the
  official number changes — your plan's papers state yours).
- Skipping D when you had no other creditable drug coverage adds a
  permanent late penalty ([see enrollment windows](02-enrollment-windows.md)).
- People with good drug coverage from a current or former employer or a
  union may reasonably wait — the key word is **creditable coverage**,
  and HR can confirm it in writing.

## Who pays what for a pretend hospital stay

Every number below is invented for teaching. Real numbers vary by plan,
state, and year — always from your own papers.

| Event | Original Medicare (no supplement) | Advantage (Plan Alpha, teaching) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Approved hospital stay of $10,000, 4 days, formally admitted | You pay the Part A deductible (imagine $1,500); Medicare pays the rest | You pay the plan's per-day copay: 4 × $350 = $1,400 |
| Follow-up doctor visit, approved $150 | You pay 20% = $30 | You pay the $15 copay |
| The same year, a second $10,000 stay within 60 days of discharge | No new deductible in the same benefit period | Copays again; but everything counts toward the plan's out-of-pocket maximum |
| Worst case this year | No cap — 20% of any Part B bills, open-ended | Capped at the plan's out-of-pocket max (imagine $6,000) |

## What each part does not do

- **Part A does not cover long-term custodial care** — daily help with
  bathing and dressing for years is not a Medicare benefit under either
  door.
- **Part B does not cover most dental, hearing aids, or routine vision.**
- **Part C is not in addition to Original Medicare** — when you are in an
  Advantage plan, Medicare itself is behind the scenes, but the plan
  administers your care.
- **Part D does not cover all drugs** — some plans exclude certain drugs
  entirely (the formulary says so), and some drug types fall outside
  typical D coverage.
