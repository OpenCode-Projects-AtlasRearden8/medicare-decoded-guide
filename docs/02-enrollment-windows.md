# 2. Enrollment Windows, Calmly

## The promise of this chapter

Medicare enrollment *sounds* like a panic-factory: TV ads warn that you are
about to lose something, phone calls insist today is the last day, and the
mail uses four exclamation points per paragraph. The truth is calmer.

**There are only a handful of windows, they come back every year, and
almost every mistake is fixable.** This chapter gives you the whole calendar
on one page — no panic, no sales, just dates.

*Education and paperwork help only — never medical, legal, financial, or
plan advice.*

## The four windows everyone should know

| Window | When it happens | What it is for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Initial Enrollment Period (IEP)** | The 7 months around your 65th birthday: 3 months before your birthday month, the birthday month itself, and 3 months after | Your first, unhurried chance to sign up for Parts A and B and pick your door |
| **Fall Open Enrollment** (also called Annual Enrollment) | October 15 – December 7, every year | The yearly shopping season: switch Advantage plans, switch drug plans, or move between the two doors; changes start January 1 |
| **Advantage Open Enrollment** | January 1 – March 31, every year | One change per year for people already in an Advantage plan: switch to another Advantage plan, or undo it by returning to Original Medicare (plus a drug plan) |
| **Special Enrollment Periods (SEPs)** | Whenever a life event triggers them | The catch-all safety net: moving, losing other coverage, qualifying for Extra Help, and many more |

Write those four on one index card and you have absorbed 90% of Medicare
enrollment law. Two more windows apply only in specific situations, below.

## The Initial Enrollment Period, slowly

The IEP is the big one, because *late* is the thing people fear. In plain
words:

- Your window opens **3 months before your birthday month** and stays open
  through the birthday month and **3 months after**.
- **Sign up in the early part of the window** (the three months before, or
  during, your birthday month) and coverage can begin on the first day of
  your birthday month.
- **Sign up later in the window** and the start date slides — usually a
  month or two later. Not a disaster, just a delay.
- Your 65th birthday is the usual trigger. If a birthday falls on the
  first of the month, the start date can land on the last day of the
  previous month — a quirk worth double-checking with Social Security if
  it's yours.

The point: **you do not have to sprint.** Three months of runway is the
federal design; you can literally spend the whole window deciding and come
out fine.

## Working past 65? The employer-coverage detour

If you (or a spouse) have health insurance through current work, you can
often wait to take Part B without penalty — but this is the classic
confusion zone, so the rules of thumb:

- The special enrollment for employer coverage generally runs while you
  have that coverage, plus **8 months after** it ends.
- **Ask HR two questions in writing:** "Does our plan expect Medicare
  first or second?" and "Do I need Part B now to avoid a penalty later?"
  Answers vary by employer size and plan type.
- You can keep the employer route and still [pick your door later](04-advantage-vs-supplement.md)
  during a later window.

## The two situational windows

- **General Enrollment Period (Jan 1 – March 31).** For people who missed
  their IEP and have no other coverage. Coverage starts July 1, and a
  late penalty may attach. It is the safety valve, not the goal.
- **Medigap Open Enrollment (6 months starting when Part B begins).** The
  one time you can buy a supplement without health questions. Outside it,
  insurers may underwrite you (decline or price by health history),
  though a few states add extra rights — ask SHIP for yours.

## Late penalties, in round numbers

Two penalties scare people. Both are small monthly add-ons that last
**forever**, and both are usually avoidable by enrolling when first
eligible or keeping other creditable coverage:

- **Part B:** roughly **10% added** for each full 12 months you went
  without B when you could have had it (and had no other coverage).
  Example in teaching numbers: wait 2 full years and pay about 20% more
  on your B premium, every month, for life.
- **Part D:** roughly **1% of a national base premium per month** late.
  In teaching numbers: skip a year and pay about 12% more on your drug
  premium's baseline, every month, for life.

Exact official numbers change and depend on your situation — Social
Security will calculate yours. The takeaway is the shape: **a small
permanent surcharge, almost always avoidable, never a cliff.**

## Special Enrollment Periods: the safety net

SEPs are the "life happened" windows. Common triggers (each with its own
length, typically 2–8 months):

- Losing employer or union coverage (your own or a spouse's)
- Moving out of a plan's service area
- Being released from jail or prison
- Qualifying for **Extra Help** or Medicaid (these come with year-round
  special rights — see [Extra Help and Other Lifelines](06-extra-help-programs.md))
- Plan changes beyond your control (plan leaves the area, loses stars
  status, contract ends)

When in doubt, the sentence to say on the phone is:

> "I think I may qualify for a Special Enrollment Period. Can you check
> what windows are open for my situation?"

## The panic killers

Re-read these whenever the ads get loud:

1. **Fall Open Enrollment comes back every single year.** October 15 –
   December 7. If you pick a plan today and regret it in April, there are
   real windows to change course.
2. **An Advantage Open Enrollment each January** gives people in
   Advantage plans one clean undo.
3. **You can never be penalized for taking two weeks to read paperwork.**
   There is no 24-hour Medicare deadline that matters.
4. **A call to 1-800-MEDICARE or a free SHIP counselor** settles "what am
   I in and when can I change it?" in one conversation — with zero
   commission pressure, because SHIP sells nothing.
5. **Nothing on television is a deadline.** Deadlines live on paper from
   Medicare and Social Security, and now on one page: yours.
