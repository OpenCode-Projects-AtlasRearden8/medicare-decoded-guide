# 4. Advantage vs. Supplement, in Round Numbers

## Two doors, honestly compared

Every person on Medicare eventually stands between **Door 1 — Original
Medicare plus a Medigap supplement** (plus a drug plan) and **Door 2 — a
Medicare Advantage plan**. Salespeople push one door. This guide pushes
neither. It compares the two in round numbers so you can see the shape of
the trade, collect your own facts, and confirm with a free SHIP counselor
before deciding.

**This chapter never recommends a plan.** It compares categories, using
made-up teaching plans — "Plan Alpha" (Advantage) and "Plan Beta"
(Original + Medigap G + a drug plan) — so no brochure can be mistaken
for a product.

*Education and paperwork help only — never medical, legal, financial, or
plan advice.*

## Table 1 — the headline trade-offs

| Question | Advantage (Plan Alpha, teaching) | Original + Medigap (Plan Beta, teaching) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly plan premium | Often $0 on top of Part B | Supplement $140 + drug plan $35, on top of Part B |
| Which doctors | Must mostly use the plan's network | Any doctor or hospital that takes Medicare, nationwide |
| Referrals needed | Often yes (HMOs) | No |
| Prior authorization | Common — plan approves services first | Rare — Medicare's own rules apply |
| Predictability of costs | Copay schedule, but bills vary with use | High — after premiums, most costs are covered; the 20% is paid by the supplement |
| Yearly out-of-pocket maximum | Required — a hard ceiling (plans state theirs) | No cap needed in theory; the supplement absorbs the 20%, leaving premiums plus deductibles |
| Dental/vision/hearing extras | Often included (varies, changes yearly) | Not included — separate purchases |
| Travel/moving | Tied to the plan's service area; emergencies covered anywhere | Works anywhere in the U.S. that accepts Medicare |
| Switching flexibility | Yearly windows; networks can change each year | Medigap plans can change brands yearly; outside open enrollment, buying a supplement usually requires health underwriting |

## Table 2 — a pretend year with made-up numbers

Imagine a quiet year and a rough year for the same made-up person.
**Every number is invented.** Real prices vary enormously by state, age,
plan, and year.

| Event in the year | Plan Alpha (Advantage) | Plan Beta (Original + Medigap G + Part D) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Monthly plan premiums | $0 | $175 ($140 + $35) = $2,100/year |
| 10 primary visits | $15 each = $150 | $0 (the supplement pays the 20%) |
| 5 specialist visits | $45 each = $225 | $0 |
| Small lab work | $0 (covered) | $0 |
| Part B deductible, paid once a year | (n/a — copays instead) | $250 |
| One 3-day hospital stay | $350/day × 3 = $1,050 | $0 (G covers the A deductible) |
| 12 drug fills | 12 × $40 = $480 | 6 × $10 + 6 × $55 = $390 |
| **Quiet-year total** | **$1,905** | **$2,740** |
| Rough-year total (several hospitalizations, more specialists) | climbs toward the plan's out-of-pocket max (imagine $6,000) | about $2,900 — premiums plus small deductibles, because the supplement keeps paying the 20% no matter how many specialists show up |

Read the last two rows as a pair, because that is the whole trade:

- **Alpha is usually cheaper in a quiet year** and can be much more
  expensive in a rough year, but never above its ceiling.
- **Beta costs more every month regardless** and is far steadier when a
  bad year happens, because there is no 20% rolling meter.

## Table 3 — what "$0 premium" actually buys

| The promise | The fine print behind it |
| --- | --- |
| "$0 monthly premium" | You still pay Part B, and copays on use. Cheap months can be offset by expensive visits. |
| "$0 deductible" | Hospital and drug costs often carry per-day copays instead. |
| "Extra benefits included" | Often capped allowances or networks with limited providers; read the Summary of Benefits rows, not the headline. |
| "Free gym membership" | Real, but usually the smallest financial element on the page. |

A $0 premium plan is a real, legitimate product — the point is only that
**the premium is one line item, not the price of the year.** That is why
[Reading a Plan's Fine Print](05-reading-fine-print.md) exists.

## The Medigap half, explained plainly

If Door 1 appeals, the supplement side has its own rules:

- **Letter plans, standardized.** A "Plan G" from one insurer covers the
  same things as a Plan G from any other. Only price and service differ —
  so the same letter can be shopped like a commodity.
- **Plan F note:** F (and high-deductible F) is generally only available
  to people who became eligible for Medicare before 2020; Plan G is the
  common equivalent for everyone else. SHIP can confirm what applies to
  you.
- **Prices vary by age, ZIP code, and insurer** — sometimes wildly for
  the identical letter. It pays to compare several.
- **The 6-month open enrollment** (starting when Part B begins) is when
  insurers must sell you any plan without health questions. Outside it,
  underwriting can decline you — which is why the
  [windows chapter](02-enrollment-windows.md) calls it the one-way door
  worth knowing about.
- **Supplements do not work with Advantage plans.** One door at a time.

## Who tends to pick which door (patterns, not advice)

- People who value **steady, predictable costs** and **keeping any
  doctor** often lean toward Original + Medigap.
- People who value **low monthly premiums** and like the plan's network
  and extras often lean toward Advantage.
- People who **travel between states for months at a time** often weigh
  Door 1's nationwide acceptance.
- People with **low incomes** should look at [Extra Help and Other
  Lifelines](06-extra-help-programs.md) first — the math changes
  completely, and in a good way.

Patterns are not recommendations. Your drugs, your doctors, your budget,
and your paperwork tolerance are the real inputs — which is what the
[decision worksheet](worksheets/decision-worksheet-advantage-or-supplement.md)
collects, and what a **SHIP counselor** walks through with you for free,
with no product to sell.

## The one-sentence summary

Advantage trades **flexibility for low monthly cost**, Medigap trades
**higher monthly cost for predictability**, and the right answer is a
personal arithmetic problem — not an ad.
